\section{Applications of inner product spaces}
\textbf{Two conventions matter.} The book uses $W$ to multiply
\emph{residuals}, so weighted least squares minimizes
$\|W(y-X\beta)\|^2$. Therefore its normal matrix contains
$W^TW=W^2$ when $W$ is diagonal. Do not replace this by $W$
unless the supplied weights have first been square-rooted.
For Fourier approximations, the inner product is the ordinary integral
$\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^{2\pi}fg$; the constant coefficient is
$a_0/2$, with $a_0=(1/\pi)\int f$, while
$a_k=(1/\pi)\int f\cos kt$ and $b_k=(1/\pi)\int f\sin kt$.
These differing denominators arise because $\|1\|^2=2\pi$ and
$\|\sin kt\|^2=\|\cos kt\|^2=\pi$ for positive integer $k$.

\practice{6.8.P1}{Check the three integral products}
For $q_1=1$, $q_2=t$, $q_3=3t^2-4$ on $[-2,2]$,
\[
 \langle q_1,q_2\rangle=\int_{-2}^2t\,dt=0,
\]
\[
 \langle q_1,q_3\rangle=[t^3-4t]_{-2}^2=0,
\]
\[
 \langle q_2,q_3\rangle=\int_{-2}^2(3t^3-4t)\,dt=0.
\]
The first and third vanish also by oddness. All three polynomials
are nonzero, so the pairwise zero products prove an orthogonal set.

\practice{6.8.P2}{Read approximations from a finite trigonometric expression}
The function is already
$f(t)=3-2\sin t+5\sin2t-6\cos2t$.
The first-order space retains only the constant and frequency-one
terms, so its approximation is $3-2\sin t$.
The third-order space contains all the displayed terms (there are no
frequency-three terms to add), so its approximation is $f$ itself.
Orthogonality makes the discarded higher-frequency terms perpendicular
to the lower-order space; this is why simply retaining terms gives
the least-squares approximation here.

\exercise{6.8.1}{Half-size residual multipliers at the two endpoints}
Use design rows $(1,x_i)$ for $x_i=-2,-1,0,1,2$ and
$y=(0,0,2,4,4)^T$. Following the book's convention, take
$W=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,2,2,1)$: the endpoint residuals receive
half the multiplier of the interior ones. Scaling all multipliers
by a common positive factor does not change the fit.
The weighted normal equations are
\[
 X^TW^2X\widehat\beta=X^TW^2y,
 \qquad
 \begin{bmatrix}14&0\\0&16\end{bmatrix}\widehat\beta
 =\begin{bmatrix}28\\24\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Thus $\widehat\beta_0=2$ and $\widehat\beta_1=3/2$.
\answer{The fitted line is $y=2+(3/2)x$. Here ``half'' refers to
the residual multiplier used in the text, whose square enters the objective.}

\exercise{6.8.2}{A common rescaling leaves the minimizers unchanged}
The second weighting matrix is $W_2=2W_1$, because every point's
multiplier is doubled. For any parameter vector,
\[
 \|W_2(y-X\beta)\|^2=4\|W_1(y-X\beta)\|^2.
\]
Multiplication by the positive constant $4$ preserves every ordering
of objective values, so the minimizing parameter vectors are exactly
the same. The numerical minimum changes by that factor, but the
fitted curve does not. This also holds when the minimizer is not unique.

\exercise{6.8.3}{Add the cubic trend to the quadratic trend}
Use nodes $-2,-1,0,1,2$ and observations $g=(3,5,5,4,3)$.
The orthogonal polynomials from Example 2 and the question are
$p_0=1,p_1=t,p_2=t^2-2,p_3=(5t^3-17t)/6$.
Their inner products with the data and squared norms are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
 j&0&1&2&3\\\hline
 \langle g,p_j\rangle&20&-1&-7&2\\
 \langle p_j,p_j\rangle&5&10&14&10
\end{array}
\]
The cubic values are $(-1,2,0,-2,1)$, so its coefficient is
$2/10=1/5$. The fitted cubic trend is
\[
 \widehat g=4p_0-\tfrac1{10}p_1-\tfrac12p_2+\tfrac15p_3
 =\boxed{5-\tfrac23t-\tfrac12t^2+\tfrac16t^3}.
\]
Because the basis is orthogonal, adding the cubic term does not require
recomputing the earlier three coefficients.

\exercise{6.8.4}{Orthogonal trends at six evenly spaced nodes}
\step{(a) Check the evaluation vectors.}
At $-5,-3,-1,1,3,5$, the proposed polynomials have lists
\[
 p_0:(1,1,1,1,1,1),\quad p_1:(-5,-3,-1,1,3,5),
\]
\[
 p_2:(5,-1,-4,-4,-1,5).
\]
The lists for $p_1$ and $p_2$ each sum to zero, so both are
orthogonal to $p_0$. Their mutual product sums to zero by odd-even
cancellation. Their squared norms are $6,70,84$, all positive.
Thus they form the required orthogonal basis of the quadratic space.
\step{(b) Project the actual data onto that basis.}
For $g=(1,1,4,4,6,8)$, the three inner products are $24,50,6$.
Hence
\[
 \widehat g=\frac{24}{6}p_0+\frac{50}{70}p_1+\frac6{84}p_2
 =4+\frac57t+\frac1{14}\left(\frac38t^2-\frac{35}{8}\right).
\]
Equivalently the quadratic is
$\boxed{\widehat g(t)=59/16+(5/7)t+(3/112)t^2}$.
The projection residual is orthogonal to all three basis lists, verifying the fit.

\exercise{6.8.5}{Sines of distinct integer frequencies are orthogonal}
For unequal positive integers $m,n$, use
$2\sin mt\sin nt=\cos((m-n)t)-\cos((m+n)t)$.
Both $m-n$ and $m+n$ are nonzero integers. Their cosine integrals
on $[0,2\pi]$ are zero, since
$\int_0^{2\pi}\cos(kt)\,dt=[\sin(kt)/k]_0^{2\pi}=0$.
Thus the integral of the original product is zero, proving orthogonality.

\exercise{6.8.6}{Sine and cosine terms are orthogonal even at equal frequency}
Use $2\sin mt\cos nt=\sin((m+n)t)+\sin((m-n)t)$.
Every nonzero integer-frequency sine integrates to zero over $[0,2\pi]$.
If $m=n$, the second term is identically $\sin0=0$, so it also
has zero integral without any division by zero. Thus
$\langle\sin mt,\cos nt\rangle=0$ for all the stated positive integers.

\exercise{6.8.7}{The Fourier basis elements have squared norm pi}
For positive integer $k$, the identities
$\cos^2kt=(1+\cos2kt)/2$ and
$\sin^2kt=(1-\cos2kt)/2$ give
\[
 \|\cos kt\|^2=\tfrac12(2\pi)+0=\pi,\qquad
 \|\sin kt\|^2=\tfrac12(2\pi)-0=\pi.
\]
The Fourier setting uses integer frequencies. For an arbitrary noninteger
real $k$, the boundary term $\sin(4\pi k)/(4k)$ need not vanish,
so the integer-frequency condition is what justifies these equalities.

\exercise{6.8.8}{A constant shift changes only the constant coefficient}
For $f(t)=t-1$, the mean coefficient is
$(1/(2\pi))\int_0^{2\pi}(t-1)\,dt=\pi-1$.
For $k\ge1$, integration by parts gives $a_k=0$ and $b_k=-2/k$;
the constant $-1$ has zero product with those sines and cosines.
\answer{The third-order approximation is
$\pi-1-2\sin t-\sin2t-(2/3)\sin3t$.}

\exercise{6.8.9}{Reverse the slope of the linear function}
For $f(t)=2\pi-t$, the constant coefficient is $\pi$.
The nonconstant Fourier coefficients of $t$ have their signs reversed:
$a_k=0$, $b_k=2/k$. These also follow directly by integrating
$(2\pi-t)\sin kt$ by parts.
\answer{The third-order approximation is
$\pi+2\sin t+\sin2t+(2/3)\sin3t$.}

\exercise{6.8.10}{Project an integrable square wave onto continuous trigonometric functions}
The square wave in the exercise has a jump at $\pi$, so it is not
literally a member of $C[0,2\pi]$. Nevertheless it is bounded and
Riemann integrable, as are all its products with the finite continuous
trigonometric basis. The finite-dimensional least-squares problem
$\min_g\int_0^{2\pi}(f-g)^2$ is therefore well defined on that
continuous trigonometric subspace. Its normal equations use the
same integrals; no assertion about an inner product on all pointwise
piecewise-continuous functions is needed. Values assigned at isolated
jump endpoints do not change any of these integrals.
The mean is zero. For $k\ge1$,
\[
 a_k=\frac1\pi\left(\int_0^\pi\cos kt\,dt
 -\int_\pi^{2\pi}\cos kt\,dt\right)=0,
\]
\[
 b_k=\frac1\pi\left(\int_0^\pi\sin kt\,dt
 -\int_\pi^{2\pi}\sin kt\,dt\right)
 =\frac{2(1-(-1)^k)}{\pi k}.
\]
Thus even sine coefficients vanish and odd ones equal $4/(\pi k)$.
\answer{The third-order approximation is
$(4/\pi)(\sin t+(1/3)\sin3t)$.}

\exercise{6.8.11}{An exact finite trigonometric identity}
The identity $\sin^2t=(1-\cos2t)/2$ already expresses the function
in the third-order target subspace. It is therefore its own best
approximation, with zero error. Its only nonzero coefficients are
the constant $1/2$ and the cosine-two coefficient $-1/2$.
\answer{$1/2-(1/2)\cos2t$. No integration is necessary.}

\exercise{6.8.12}{Use the cosine triple-angle identity}
From $\cos3t=4\cos^3t-3\cos t$, solve for the cube:
$\cos^3t=(3/4)\cos t+(1/4)\cos3t$.
This lies entirely in the third-order target space, so the best
approximation is exact, again with zero error.
\answer{$(3/4)\cos t+(1/4)\cos3t$.}

\exercise{6.8.13}{Each coefficient depends linearly on the function}
For example,
\[
 a_k(f+g)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}(f+g)\cos kt
 =a_k(f)+a_k(g)
\]
by linearity of the integral. Replacing cosine by sine proves the
same identity for $b_k$, and taking the constant term proves it
for $a_0$ too. The fixed denominators are the same for both functions,
so no extra normalization appears when adding coefficients.

\exercise{6.8.14}{One additional correct Fourier coefficient cannot worsen the fit}
The difference is $h-g=b_2\sin2t$. Since $b_2$ is the Fourier
coefficient of $f$, the residual $f-h$ is orthogonal to $\sin2t$.
Therefore the Pythagorean theorem gives
\[
 \|f-g\|^2=\|(f-h)+b_2\sin2t\|^2
 =\|f-h\|^2+\pi b_2^2.
\]
\answer{$h$ is at least as close as $g$. It is strictly closer if
$b_2\ne0$; if $b_2=0$, the two functions and their errors are identical.}

\exercise{6.8.16}{Graph two Fourier orders and their periodic continuation}
From Exercise 10, even sine coefficients vanish, so
\[
 f_4(t)=\frac4\pi\left(\sin t+\frac{\sin3t}{3}\right),\qquad
 f_5(t)=f_4(t)+\frac4{5\pi}\sin5t.
\]
Thus the fourth-order projection equals the third-order projection:
its new frequency-four coefficients are zero. The fifth adds a
nonzero frequency-five term. The requested three separate plots follow;
the formulas determine the values exactly, while the curves are sampled
densely for drawing. Both are $2\pi$-periodic, explaining the
continuation on the third plot.
