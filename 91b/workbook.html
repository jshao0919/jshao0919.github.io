Lesson 1 / 14 · 20 minutes

Start with the vehicle and its manual

Understand what a 91B does, where PMCS fits, and how to find the instruction that applies to the vehicle in front of you.

Learning objectives

  • Separate operator checks from maintainer work and release authority.
  • Match equipment identity to the correct technical publication.
  • Read a PMCS table without confusing a check with a rejection criterion.

What PMCS accomplishes

Preventive maintenance checks and services (PMCS) are organized inspections and services used to find problems early, preserve equipment, and document its condition. A check asks you to look, listen, measure, or verify something. A service directs an action such as lubrication or replacement when the publication authorizes it. A quick walk around is useful, but it is not automatically a complete PMCS. The applicable technical manual (TM) tells you what work is required and when.

  1. Identify the vehicle and the inspection interval you are performing.
  2. Use the applicable PMCS table in its stated order.
  3. Perform each authorized check using its instructions and precautions.
  4. Record findings that require documentation and report conditions that prevent safe completion.
Worked example

Seeing that a tire is present does not complete a tire check. The TM may require condition, inflation, wheel hardware, and other observations using specified methods.

Operator, maintainer, and supervisor roles

An operator conducts the checks and services assigned to the operator and reports faults. A 91B wheeled vehicle mechanic inspects, troubleshoots, repairs, and services equipment within training, authorization, and the maintenance allocation. A supervisor coordinates work, reviews documentation, and applies the relevant maintenance controls. A dispatch or release decision belongs to the designated authority. Finding a problem does not automatically authorize a repair, a status change, or operation of the vehicle.

  1. Read who the procedure is written for and what maintenance level it assigns.
  2. Check that required training, tools, and authorization are available.
  3. Refer work outside your assignment to the appropriate maintainer or supervisor.
  4. Treat completion of a repair and authorization to operate as separate decisions.

Identify the equipment before opening a procedure

A family name or familiar appearance may not identify the exact configuration. Model, serial or registration identifiers, installed equipment, and applicable modifications can change the instruction. Use equipment records and identification plates according to local procedure. Do not infer an exact model from a generic drawing in this course. The truck in the demonstrations is an illustration, not a vehicle-specific maintenance reference.

  1. Read the equipment identification required by your work package or record.
  2. Match the model and configuration to the publication applicability statement.
  3. Check publication date, changes, warnings, and relevant supplemental instructions.
  4. If identity or applicability is uncertain, resolve that uncertainty before performing the procedure.

Read the whole PMCS entry

PMCS tables commonly separate an item number, interval, location or item to check, procedure, and a column describing conditions under which equipment is not ready or available. Titles vary by manual. Read across the entire row, including notes and references. An item number is a navigation aid: it is not a vehicle fault code or a universal reference shared by every TM.

  1. Locate the interval and the item in the correct table.
  2. Read warnings, cautions, and notes before starting.
  3. Follow the procedure and any referenced work package.
  4. Compare the actual observation with the applicable criteria.
  5. Record the correct reference and observation; escalate uncertainty instead of inventing a limit.
Worked example

“Inspect for leaks” tells you what to observe. The associated criteria and fluid-system instructions determine what the finding means for that vehicle.

Prepare a reference trail

A useful record lets another person find the instruction you used. Keep the publication identifier, applicable change information, and relevant table, work package, or item available. Use the exact reference format required by the form and local process. This course supplies general examples and links to publication discovery pages; it does not verify the currently effective edition of every Army publication.

  1. Obtain the authorized current publication through your unit’s publication process.
  2. Keep the relevant instruction available at the job, rather than relying on memory.
  3. Record the required publication identification accurately.
  4. When the form and the TM appear inconsistent, ask the maintenance supervisor to resolve the conflict.

Keep these habits

  • The applicable vehicle TM supplies the actual procedure and limits.
  • A mechanic’s skill does not replace authorization or maintenance allocation.
  • Read every PMCS row as a complete instruction, including its criteria.

Read alongside: The technical manual for the exact vehicle and configuration; AR 750–1 — Army Materiel Maintenance Policy; DA Pam 750–1 — maintenance management guidance.

Lesson 2 / 14 · 25 minutes

Set up a safe inspection

Prepare the vehicle, work area, people, and tools before reaching into a system or moving the vehicle.

Learning objectives

  • Recognize stored energy and movement hazards.
  • Use the procedure to choose the safe vehicle state.
  • Explain when to stop an inspection and obtain help.

Start with a work-area scan

Before touching the vehicle, look at the ground, surrounding traffic, overhead space, ventilation, lighting, and escape routes. Loose tools, oil, water, and poor lighting can turn a routine check into an injury. Keep other people out of the immediate work area when the procedure requires it. Select protective equipment for the actual task and exposure; gloves alone do not protect against every chemical, rotating component, or electrical hazard.

  1. Identify the work area and control traffic or pedestrian access as required.
  2. Check lighting, footing, ventilation, and the location of emergency equipment.
  3. Remove or report hazards that can be corrected safely.
  4. Gather task-appropriate protective equipment and inspect it.
  5. Tell the responsible person what work you are beginning.

Establish the required vehicle state

The safe state depends on the check. Some checks require the engine off and the system cool; others require a controlled operating check by authorized personnel. Never assume that parking alone removes all energy. A vehicle can roll, a raised component can fall, a system can retain pressure, and electrical circuits can remain energized. Follow the TM and applicable energy-control procedure for the specific task.

  1. Read the procedure’s initial setup and warnings.
  2. Secure the vehicle against movement as instructed.
  3. Control ignition, electrical power, pressure, and other energy sources as required.
  4. Verify the specified safe condition before beginning hands-on work.
  5. Restore power or movement only through the prescribed sequence and communication.
Worked example

An engine-off visual inspection and an engine-running observation have different boundaries. Do not carry an engine-off hand placement into a running check.

Respect heat, pressure, and moving parts

Hot coolant, oil, exhaust surfaces, and pressurized systems can cause serious injury. Do not loosen a hot cooling-system cap, open a line to “see if it has pressure,” or search for a pressurized leak with your hand. Keep clothing, hair, jewelry, and tools clear of fans, belts, shafts, and other moving parts. Some components can start or move unexpectedly. Use the inspection method and tools the procedure specifies.

  1. Identify heat, pressure, rotation, and automatic movement hazards before approaching.
  2. Wait for or establish the conditions specified by the procedure.
  3. Use an approved observation position; do not defeat guards.
  4. Stop if an unexpected spray, movement, smell, or temperature condition appears.
  5. Report suspected injection or other serious injury immediately through emergency procedures.

Lifting, wheels, and stored mechanical energy

A jack is a lifting device, not a substitute for the specified support arrangement. Never go beneath an unsupported vehicle or raised load. Wheels and tires can contain substantial stored energy, and some assemblies require specialized training and equipment. Springs, raised bodies, and other mechanisms also store energy. This course teaches recognition and reporting; it does not provide a lifting, tire-disassembly, or suspension-disassembly procedure.

  1. Identify whether the task requires lifting or work beneath a raised component.
  2. Verify that an authorized procedure, correct equipment, and trained personnel are available.
  3. Use designated lifting and support points only as the TM directs.
  4. Keep clear until the required support and energy controls have been verified.
  5. Refer specialized wheel, tire, and spring work to qualified personnel.

Use a stop-and-report decision

Stopping is the correct action when the procedure cannot be followed safely, required equipment is unavailable, or a condition may prevent safe operation. First protect people and prevent further exposure. Then identify the vehicle, describe what you observed, and notify the responsible person. Do not make an uncertain situation “fit” the checklist by checking a box or entering a guessed result.

  1. Pause the task without creating an additional hazard.
  2. Secure the immediate area using the applicable procedure.
  3. Notify the supervisor or other designated authority.
  4. Record what was found and which check could not be completed.
  5. Resume only after the issue is resolved and the work is authorized.

Keep these habits

  • Choose the vehicle state from the procedure, not habit.
  • An engine-off vehicle may still contain pressure, heat, electricity, or stored mechanical energy.
  • A check that cannot be completed safely must be reported accurately.

Read alongside: The technical manual for the exact vehicle and configuration; DA Pam 750–1 — maintenance management guidance.

Lesson 3 / 14 · 30 minutes

Before-operation PMCS: build a repeatable walkaround

Follow a consistent inspection route while using the correct manual, not a memorized generic checklist.

Learning objectives

  • Prepare records and inspect the vehicle systematically.
  • Distinguish an observation from a diagnosis.
  • Document incomplete checks and conditions requiring review.

Review the record before inspecting

A maintenance worksheet gives context: existing faults, open work, and equipment identification may already be recorded. Review it with the appropriate person before starting. An existing entry does not excuse you from checking the condition, and an empty fault section does not prove the vehicle is serviceable. Verify what work or limitations are currently applicable through the authorized maintenance process.

  1. Confirm the worksheet belongs to the vehicle in front of you.
  2. Review open faults and any applicable restrictions with the responsible person.
  3. Identify the before-operation PMCS instructions and required materials.
  4. Resolve conflicting identity or status information before continuing.
  5. Set up the vehicle safely for the first check.

Use a route to prevent omissions

A consistent route helps you notice changes and avoid checking the same item twice while missing another. The TM’s order controls; the course’s front, side, rear, cab, and underbody zones are only a learning aid. Pause at each item, observe it deliberately, and record the result before moving on. Do not crawl into an unsafe location just to match the illustration.

  1. Locate the first PMCS item and establish a safe viewing position.
  2. Inspect that item using the required method.
  3. Check the associated criteria and note any fault.
  4. Move to the next item in the table.
  5. At the end, review the table for missed or incomplete items.
Worked example

You observe a wet area near the left rear of a vehicle. Record the location and appearance, then use the TM to determine the authorized next check. Do not immediately label it a failed axle seal.

Exterior and ground observations

Before starting the vehicle, inspect the surrounding ground and visible exterior components as directed. New puddles, disturbed hardware, damaged lenses, unsecured panels, or a change in stance can prompt closer attention. A stain on the ground may belong to a previous vehicle; a dry ground surface does not rule out a leak that occurs only during operation. Record evidence with enough location and context for a maintainer to investigate.

  1. Observe the ground without walking through an unknown spill.
  2. Check visible body, lighting, access-panel, and mounting conditions required by the TM.
  3. Inspect tires, wheels, and accessible suspension items from safe positions.
  4. Note new or changed conditions relative to existing records.
  5. Use the specific procedure before opening panels or checking fluids.

Cab and required operating checks

Inspect the cab, restraints, visibility, controls, and instruments according to the applicable instructions. Confirm that loose items are secured. If the TM requires a start or operating check, complete its prerequisites and use the required ventilation, clearance, and personnel. A dashboard lamp turning off does not establish that every related component is serviceable; the full check still matters.

  1. Inspect access, seating, restraints, and required visibility items.
  2. Identify controls and required indicators before use.
  3. Complete the TM’s prerequisites for any start or functional check.
  4. Observe the specified indication or response and compare it with the criteria.
  5. Stop and report an abnormal condition using the applicable procedure.

Close the inspection loop

The last step is a review, not an assumption that the vehicle may move. Confirm which checks were completed, which faults were recorded, and what needs supervisor or maintenance review. The person authorized to determine status or dispatch uses the applicable records and criteria. Never represent an unperformed check as complete because the vehicle is needed.

  1. Reconcile your notes with the worksheet.
  2. Ensure each fault has an accurate location, observation, and required reference.
  3. Identify checks that could not be completed and why.
  4. Report any condition requiring immediate review.
  5. Obtain the required review or dispatch action before operation.

Keep these habits

  • A repeatable route supports the TM; it does not replace the TM.
  • Review existing faults and report changes.
  • Completing a walkaround is separate from authorizing operation.

Read alongside: The technical manual for the exact vehicle and configuration; DA Pam 750–8 — The Army Maintenance Management System (TAMMS) Users Manual; Dispatch records — DA Form 5987-E / DD Form 1970, as applicable.

Lesson 4 / 14 · 25 minutes

During and after operation: notice, stop, and follow through

Observe vehicle behavior safely, record useful context, and complete the required checks after use.

Learning objectives

  • Monitor required indications without distracting the operator.
  • Describe an abnormal event with useful operating context.
  • Complete after-operation records and handoff.

During-operation PMCS is continuous attention

During-operation checks focus on required indications and vehicle behavior while the vehicle is being used. Examples include instrument indications, unusual noise, vibration, steering response, and braking behavior when the applicable procedure calls for those observations. They do not justify unsafe experiments. The operator must maintain safe control and attention; record detailed notes only when it is safe to do so.

  1. Know the required indications and operating precautions before moving.
  2. Observe the items specified by the TM during normal authorized use.
  3. Recognize a change from the expected condition.
  4. Follow the TM and operating procedure if an abnormal indication appears.
  5. Record the event once the vehicle and people are safe.

Respond to an abnormal indication

An unexpected warning, smoke, smell, loss of response, or unusual vibration requires a deliberate response. Do not continue simply to see whether it becomes worse. Use the vehicle’s published response procedure and the local emergency or recovery process as applicable. This course does not prescribe a universal shutdown sequence because the safe action depends on the system and situation.

  1. Maintain control and use the applicable immediate-action procedure.
  2. Bring the vehicle and work area to the required safe condition.
  3. Notify the responsible person and identify the vehicle.
  4. Describe the indication and circumstances without guessing a failed component.
  5. Do not resume operation until the required evaluation and authorization are complete.

Capture the context a mechanic needs

A fault that occurs only under certain conditions can be difficult to reproduce. Useful context includes when it began, what the vehicle was doing, which indication changed, and whether the condition continued after stopping. Record available readings accurately with units. Do not invent a number from memory, deliberately recreate a hazardous event, or state that a component failed when you only observed a symptom.

  1. Record the date and the relevant meter reading through the required process.
  2. Name the location, indicator, or function involved.
  3. State the operating condition in ordinary language.
  4. Record the observed effect and any action taken.
  5. Tell the maintainer what is uncertain or was not observed.
Worked example

Useful: “During normal operation, an intermittent squeal was heard near the engine compartment; source not determined. Reported to maintenance.” This is more informative than “engine bad,” without claiming an unverified cause.

After-operation PMCS has its own instructions

After use, some conditions are easier to notice, and the vehicle needs to be prepared for its next use. Follow the after-operation table rather than repeating only the checks you remember from the morning. Some observations require a particular temperature, waiting period, or vehicle state; those conditions come from the TM. Do not open hot or pressurized components simply because the vehicle has been parked.

  1. Secure the vehicle and establish the state required by the procedure.
  2. Perform the after-operation PMCS items in their specified order.
  3. Inspect for new damage, leaks, or loose items as directed.
  4. Perform only authorized replenishment, cleaning, or other services.
  5. Complete the record and report unresolved or new faults.

Make the handoff understandable

A useful handoff connects the observed condition, the record, and the next responsible person. Explain what happened, what was checked, what remains uncertain, and whether the vehicle has been referred for review. Do not rely on a verbal comment that never reaches the worksheet or maintenance process. The next operator needs the current record, not an informal promise that a problem will be fixed later.

  1. Confirm that all required observations and meter information were entered.
  2. Point out new or changed faults to the responsible person.
  3. Identify any checks or corrective actions still incomplete.
  4. Follow the unit’s record-routing and vehicle-securing process.
  5. Verify that the handoff was received.

Keep these habits

  • Monitor required indications without compromising safe operation.
  • Context helps troubleshooting; guesses make it harder.
  • After-operation checks and a documented handoff prepare the next user.

Read alongside: The technical manual for the exact vehicle and configuration; DA Pam 750–8 — The Army Maintenance Management System (TAMMS) Users Manual; Dispatch records — DA Form 5987-E / DD Form 1970, as applicable.

Tires and wheels: inspect without creating a hazard

Recognize common tire and wheel observations, use the proper inspection method, and leave specialized work to qualified personnel.

Learning objectives

  • Describe tire damage and wheel concerns precisely.
  • Explain why pressure and torque values must come from the applicable procedure.
  • Recognize wheel-assembly stored-energy hazards.

Start with position and visible condition

Identify the exact wheel position using the vehicle’s terminology. Inspect the visible tread, sidewall, valve area, and surrounding components as the TM directs. Look for the kinds of damage and interference the procedure identifies. A tire that appears inflated can still be outside its specified condition or pressure. Do not reach into a tight or unsafe space to see the hidden side of a wheel.

  1. Secure the vehicle and choose a safe viewing position.
  2. Identify the wheel position unambiguously.
  3. Inspect the accessible surfaces using the required method.
  4. Note the location and appearance of damage or contact.
  5. Use the TM’s criteria to determine the required reporting or maintenance action.

Pressure is a measurement, not an estimate

The required tire pressure and measurement conditions depend on the tire, vehicle, configuration, and procedure. Use the specified gauge and method. A generic training value is not an acceptable substitute. A central tire inflation indication, if fitted, must be interpreted under that system’s instructions. Inflation or deflation is a separate action that requires authorization and the correct safe procedure.

  1. Find the correct pressure instruction for the installed assembly and use conditions.
  2. Confirm the required measurement state and gauge suitability.
  3. Measure only as the TM and your training permit.
  4. Record the actual value and unit when required.
  5. Report a discrepancy and use the authorized adjustment or repair process.

Wheel hardware and witness marks

Inspect accessible wheel hardware and related indicators as the TM specifies. Missing hardware, visible movement, unusual staining, damaged components, or a changed witness mark can require investigation. A witness mark is only evidence of relative movement; it does not prove that the assembly has the correct torque. Do not use an improvised tightening sequence or a remembered torque value.

  1. Observe the hardware without disturbing it during a visual check.
  2. Compare the condition with the applicable inspection criteria.
  3. Record which position and component is affected.
  4. Refer required torque verification or repair to the authorized procedure and personnel.
  5. Ensure corrective work is documented and checked as required.

Treat wheel assemblies as stored-energy systems

Tire and wheel assemblies can release energy violently. Damaged wheels, multi-piece assemblies, inflation work, and disassembly require specific training and equipment. Do not loosen assembly hardware, apply heat, or attempt an improvised repair. The safe response to a suspected dangerous assembly is to keep people clear and use the established maintenance process.

  1. Recognize that inspection does not authorize disassembly.
  2. Do not approach or handle an assembly showing a condition identified as hazardous by the procedure.
  3. Keep others away from the hazard as required.
  4. Notify qualified personnel and explain the observation.
  5. Follow the approved isolation, handling, and repair instructions.

Write a tire or wheel fault that can be found

A useful entry identifies the vehicle location, component, observed condition, and applicable inspection reference. Avoid “bad tire” or “wheel loose” unless looseness was actually established by an authorized check. A drawing or photograph may supplement the record if unit rules allow it, but it does not replace the required written entry.

  1. Name the exact wheel position.
  2. State what you saw or measured.
  3. Include the required PMCS item or technical reference.
  4. Record any relevant measurement with its unit and method context.
  5. Report the condition for status determination under the TM.
Worked example

Training wording: “Left front tire sidewall has visible damage near the outer shoulder; condition referred for evaluation against the applicable TM criteria.” Add actual measurements only if the procedure required and you performed them.

Keep these habits

  • Vehicle-specific pressure and torque values come from the correct technical data.
  • Witness marks do not verify torque.
  • Do not turn a visual inspection into unauthorized tire or wheel work.

Lesson 6 / 14 · 30 minutes

Fluids, leaks, and cooling systems

Check fluids under the correct conditions, describe leakage accurately, and distinguish leak appearance from operating authority.

Learning objectives

  • Use the proper conditions and method for a level check.
  • Distinguish Class I, II, and III leak appearance.
  • Explain why leak class alone cannot determine whether a vehicle may operate.

A level reading needs the right conditions

Fluid level depends on the system and inspection conditions. The TM may specify vehicle attitude, temperature, engine state, waiting time, and how to read a gauge or indicator. A reading taken under the wrong conditions can lead to overfilling or a missed problem. Identify the exact fill or check point before opening anything. Never use the course illustration to locate a real component.

  1. Identify the system and applicable level-check instruction.
  2. Establish the specified vehicle, temperature, and engine conditions.
  3. Observe warnings about heat, pressure, contamination, and moving parts.
  4. Read the correct indicator using the required method.
  5. Record or report the result and perform replenishment only if authorized.

Use only the specified product and amount

Fluids that look similar may have very different properties. Use the product specification and replenishment method required by the applicable publication. Keep containers and tools clean, protect open systems from dirt, and prevent cross-contamination. Do not mix products based on color or substitute an available lubricant without an approved instruction.

  1. Verify the required product specification and container identification.
  2. Inspect the container and dispensing equipment for contamination or damage.
  3. Use the authorized fill procedure and quantity or level criterion.
  4. Secure closures and clean incidental residue as instructed.
  5. Document consumption, service, or a recurring need for replenishment when required.

Describe leak classes carefully

A common Army leak-description convention distinguishes Class I seepage or wetness that does not form drops, Class II leakage that forms drops but does not cause them to fall, and Class III leakage in which drops fall from the item being checked. Use the definitions in the applicable publication when classifying a real finding. These descriptions concern appearance; they do not identify the fluid, locate the original source, or grant permission to operate.

  1. Observe from a safe position using the prescribed method.
  2. Determine whether there is wetness, a formed drop, or falling drops.
  3. Identify the system and source only as far as the evidence supports.
  4. Use the applicable leak definition and PMCS criteria.
  5. Record the observation and refer any uncertainty for evaluation.
Worked example

A falling drop fits the usual Class III description. Whether that condition makes this vehicle unavailable must still be evaluated using the system-specific TM criteria and required authority.

Fluid type and system matter

Do not treat every leak alike. A small amount of fluid in a safety-critical system may demand a different response from a similar-looking stain elsewhere. Fuel, brake-related fluids, coolant, lubricants, and unknown fluids have different hazards. A dark spot does not prove that the source is engine oil, and fluid can travel along a surface before it drips. Avoid touching or smelling an unknown fluid to identify it.

  1. Keep clear of hot, moving, pressurized, or otherwise hazardous areas.
  2. Record the location and appearance without guessing the source.
  3. Consult the applicable system instructions and warnings.
  4. Report an unknown or potentially hazardous leak promptly.
  5. Use the approved spill, containment, and disposal process when required.

Cooling-system checks and heat

Cooling-system inspection can include accessible hoses, connections, visible condition, and indicators when the TM directs it. A hot system may remain pressurized after shutdown. Never loosen a hot cap to speed the check, squeeze a hose to infer pressure, or continue operation based on an assumption that a warning is “just a sensor.” Fans and belts also require the specified separation and energy controls.

  1. Read the cooling-system warning and setup instructions.
  2. Wait for or establish the required safe state.
  3. Inspect the specified visible items without bypassing guards.
  4. Check the level or other condition only by the authorized method.
  5. Record a leak, damaged component, or abnormal indication and obtain the required review.

Turn a leak observation into a useful record

Separate what you directly observed from what still needs diagnosis. Include the location, fluid identity if established, leak appearance, and relevant operating conditions. If cleaning and reinspection are required to locate the source, that is a task for the authorized procedure. Do not erase the original observation merely because the surface looks dry later.

  1. Name the location as precisely as you can.
  2. State wetness, formed drops, or falling drops as observed.
  3. State the fluid identity only if established.
  4. Record the check conditions and required technical reference.
  5. Report the finding and document any authorized follow-up.
Worked example

Training wording: “Wetness visible on the lower left side of the engine compartment; no drops formed during this inspection. Fluid and source not confirmed.” This keeps observation separate from diagnosis.

Keep these habits

  • Level checks require the TM’s specified conditions.
  • Leak class describes appearance; system criteria determine the maintenance consequence.
  • Never open a hot pressurized system or identify an unknown fluid by touch.

Lesson 7 / 14 · 25 minutes

Brakes, steering, and suspension observations

Recognize what needs inspection and reporting without inventing universal adjustment or rejection limits.

Learning objectives

  • Follow the prescribed method for safety-critical checks.
  • Distinguish visual observations from authorized functional tests.
  • Report abnormal braking or steering behavior without unsafe experiments.

Begin with the applicable system

Vehicles use different brake, steering, and suspension arrangements. First identify the system and read its inspection instructions. Do not transfer an adjustment method, pressure value, travel limit, or test sequence from another vehicle. A familiar control layout does not establish that the underlying system is the same.

  1. Confirm vehicle configuration and the relevant PMCS items.
  2. Read the system’s warnings and setup requirements.
  3. Identify which checks are assigned to your role.
  4. Prepare the required equipment and safe work area.
  5. Refer unclear instructions or conflicting symptoms to a qualified maintainer.

Inspect accessible components safely

Visual checks may address accessible lines, hoses, connections, mountings, and other components identified in the TM. Look for the specified kinds of damage, leakage, interference, or missing hardware. Do not place yourself under an unsupported vehicle or put fingers between components that can move. A visual check cannot establish an internal condition that requires a specified test.

  1. Establish the required safe vehicle state.
  2. Use a safe viewing position for each specified component.
  3. Observe condition without disturbing an adjustment or opening a line.
  4. Record the exact location and visible condition.
  5. Refer required measurements or tests to the applicable procedure.

Conduct only the prescribed functional check

A functional check asks whether a system responds as specified under controlled conditions. It may require an operator, an observer, a prepared area, or particular initial conditions. Do not improvise a road test, defeat a warning, or adjust a component until a symptom disappears. A successful response during one check does not cancel an unresolved fault from another check.

  1. Read the complete check, including its stop criteria, before starting.
  2. Confirm the required personnel, area, and initial conditions.
  3. Perform the specified actions in order.
  4. Compare the observed response with the stated criteria.
  5. Stop and report any unexpected or unacceptable response.

Describe the symptom without prescribing a repair

Examples of useful observations include a specific warning indication, visible leakage at a stated location, unusual steering effort, or a reported braking change. “Replace the pump” is a repair recommendation, not a description of what the operator observed. Diagnosis requires the authorized troubleshooting process and may reveal a different cause.

  1. Identify the affected function and the observed symptom.
  2. Include when the symptom occurred and what was happening.
  3. Record actual readings only when obtained by the required method.
  4. Avoid naming a failed part unless it has been confirmed.
  5. Report the condition for maintenance evaluation and status determination.
Worked example

Training wording: “Operator reports steering effort increased during authorized operation; cause not determined. Vehicle referred for maintenance evaluation.”

Do not use a defect symbol as permission

Recording a status symbol communicates a condition through the maintenance system; it does not independently authorize movement. Use the applicable TM criteria, current forms instructions, and designated review process. Do not reduce the severity of an entry because a short movement seems convenient or because the vehicle completed its last trip.

  1. Document the observed condition accurately.
  2. Apply the required criteria and obtain review when uncertain.
  3. Keep operation within the current authorized status and restrictions.
  4. Complete authorized repair and required verification.
  5. Have the designated personnel complete any status or release action.

Keep these habits

  • Safety-critical systems require their own procedures and limits.
  • Functional checks need defined conditions and stop criteria.
  • A symptom is evidence for troubleshooting, not proof of a failed part.

Batteries, wiring, lights, and electrical symptoms

Inspect accessible electrical items, recognize battery hazards, and document electrical faults without unsafe trial-and-error work.

Learning objectives

  • Identify common battery and electrical inspection hazards.
  • Separate visible evidence from an electrical diagnosis.
  • Use prescribed test equipment and connection procedures.

Electrical power can remain present

Turning off a control does not necessarily isolate every circuit. Batteries, stored energy, and alternate power paths may still present hazards. Jewelry and conductive tools can create a short circuit. Follow the task’s power-control instructions and use appropriate protective equipment. Do not disconnect a cable or remove a cover merely because the engine is stopped.

  1. Read the electrical warnings and initial setup for the task.
  2. Identify the required power state and energy controls.
  3. Remove or control conductive items as the procedure requires.
  4. Use the specified tools and safe access method.
  5. Verify the required state before hands-on work.

Battery inspection begins with condition and restraint

Battery tasks can involve corrosive material, gas, high current, and heavy components. Inspect the items assigned by the TM, such as accessible case condition, restraint, connections, and protective covers. Avoid sparks and ignition sources. A swollen, cracked, leaking, hot, or otherwise abnormal battery requires the procedure’s response and qualified review; do not experiment with charging or connection changes.

  1. Prepare the work area and protective equipment specified for the task.
  2. Observe accessible battery and mounting conditions safely.
  3. Record damage, leakage, corrosion, or insecure components as required.
  4. Use the approved response for an abnormal battery condition.
  5. Perform cleaning, servicing, removal, or charging only under the applicable procedure and authorization.

Wiring and connections: describe what is visible

Accessible wiring checks can reveal abrasion, damaged insulation, insecure routing, missing protection, or a visibly disconnected connector. Do not pull on a harness to “test” it, pierce insulation without an authorized procedure, or reconnect an unknown connector by guesswork. Electrical faults may be intermittent, and damage location matters to troubleshooting.

  1. Identify the harness or component location using the TM terminology.
  2. Observe routing, protection, and visible condition as directed.
  3. Keep clear of energized or moving components.
  4. Record the specific visible defect and location.
  5. Refer continuity, voltage, or other testing to the authorized procedure.

Lights and indicators need a defined check

A lighting check is more useful when it states which lamp, selected function, and observed response were involved. Confirm the required switch position and test conditions using the TM. A lamp that does not illuminate may have several possible causes. Do not immediately replace parts or bypass a protective device without troubleshooting and authorization.

  1. Prepare the vehicle and observer position for the specified check.
  2. Select the required function according to the procedure.
  3. Observe each required lamp or indication.
  4. Record the exact function and location that did not respond correctly.
  5. Follow the approved troubleshooting and repair process.
Worked example

Training wording: “Right rear turn indicator did not illuminate during the prescribed lighting check; left rear indicator illuminated.” This is more useful than “electrical system broken.”

Meters, protective devices, and starting support

Use test equipment with the correct rating, condition, range, and connection method specified for the task. Incorrect connections can damage equipment or injure personnel. Never bridge a fuse or circuit-protection device to make a system operate. External starting support, battery connection order, and charging procedures are vehicle- and equipment-specific tasks; use the exact authorized instructions and trained personnel.

  1. Confirm that testing or starting support is part of the authorized task.
  2. Inspect the required equipment and verify its suitability.
  3. Read the complete connection and disconnection sequence.
  4. Perform the procedure within its stated limits and precautions.
  5. Record the result and remove test equipment through the prescribed sequence.

Keep these habits

  • Engine off does not mean every electrical circuit is safe to touch.
  • Report the exact lamp, harness location, or indication involved.
  • Do not bypass protection or improvise battery and test-equipment connections.

Scheduled services and the maintenance workflow

Intervals are instructions, not suggestions

Before, during, after, weekly, monthly, and service-based intervals appear only where the applicable publication requires them. From observation to work assignment

A fault begins as evidence: a visible condition, a measured result, or an operating symptom. The record then supports review, prioritization, and assignment. The exact digital and paper workflow varies by unit and system configuration. Keep the original observation understandable so the assigned maintainer does not have to reconstruct what the operator meant.

  1. Document the finding and required reference.
  2. Submit it through the unit’s maintenance reporting process.
  3. Have the designated personnel review status and work requirements.
  4. Assign work to qualified personnel using the required work documentation.
  5. Preserve the connection between the original fault and later corrective actions.

Troubleshoot before replacing parts

A troubleshooting procedure narrows possible causes through specified observations and tests. Follow its sequence and safety instructions. Replacing the most familiar part may waste time and leave the fault unresolved. Record test results accurately, including required units and conditions, so the next maintainer can understand what has been ruled in or out.

  1. Confirm the reported symptom using an authorized method when safe.
  2. Find the applicable troubleshooting procedure.
  3. Complete each required test in order with suitable equipment.

  1. Identify the required part or support through authorized technical and supply data.
  2. Record the request or work status through the designated system.
  3. Keep the fault visible until the required corrective and verification actions are complete.
  4. Use authorized deferred-maintenance records only when applicable.

Repair, verify, and close the record

Corrective action should state what was actually done. Verification should state the check performed and its result, as required by the task and records process. “Fixed” alone gives the next person little information. Required inspections, supervisor reviews, and status changes must be completed by the designated personnel. Never pre-sign a job or enter completion before the work occurs.

  1. Perform the authorized repair or service using the applicable instruction.
  2. Conduct the specified operational, quality, or other verification check.
  3. Record the actual corrective action and verification result.
  4. Obtain required reviews, signatures, or system actions from the responsible people.

  1. Account for tools and removed or replaced items.
  2. Restore the configuration specified by the procedure.
  3. Handle waste, used fluids, and contaminated materials correctly.
  4. Review the record for incomplete entries and unresolved faults.
  5. Brief the next responsible person on the final condition and remaining work.

Keep these habits

  • Use the actual schedule and meter data for the vehicle.
  • A parts request does not close a fault.
  • Repair, verification, record completion, and release are distinct steps.

Write faults that another mechanic can act on

Turn an observation into a precise entry, apply status concepts carefully, and avoid common documentation errors.

Learning objectives

  • Write a fault using location, observation, conditions, and reference.
  • Separate fault descriptions from corrective-action entries.
  • Explain common status symbols without treating them as self-authorized operating decisions.

Use four pieces of useful information

A useful fault entry answers: where is it, what was observed, under what relevant conditions, and which required inspection reference applies? It should be concise enough to read and specific enough to find. Include measurements and units only when they were actually obtained. The exact field placement depends on the form or system; the quality of the observation matters in every format.

  1. Name the component or exact vehicle location.
  2. Describe what you saw, heard, measured, or experienced.
  3. Add relevant operating or inspection conditions.
  4. Enter the required PMCS item or technical reference in the appropriate field.
  5. Read the entry as if you had never seen the vehicle.
Worked example

Weak: “Light bad.” Better: “Right rear turn indicator did not illuminate during the prescribed lighting check.” The required item reference belongs in the form’s reference field.

Keep observation separate from diagnosis

An observation is direct evidence. A diagnosis identifies the cause after the required evaluation. An operator can accurately report that a lamp did not illuminate without asserting that the bulb failed. A mechanic can later record a confirmed cause and corrective action. Keeping those stages separate prevents guesses from becoming accepted facts in the maintenance history.

  1. Underline the facts you directly observed.
  2. Remove any unverified failed-part claim.
  3. State “source not confirmed” or similar wording when needed.
  4. Let authorized troubleshooting establish the cause.
  5. Record the confirmed cause and repair in the proper corrective-action workflow.

Understand common status symbols

Common maintenance-record symbols include X for a deficiency that places equipment in an inoperable status; a diagonal slash for a material defect other than such a deficiency; and a horizontal dash for required inspection, maintenance action, or other applicable requirement that is due but not accomplished, as defined by the current instructions. A circled X concerns operation under specifically authorized limitations. Use the exact current definitions and applicable TM criteria. These brief descriptions are not a substitute for status determination or release authority.

  1. Find the condition’s applicable technical criteria.
  2. Consult the current form or system instructions for the symbol.
  3. Have the designated personnel resolve uncertain status.
  4. Document the condition accurately without selecting a less restrictive symbol for convenience.
  5. Keep any authorized limitations explicit and within the established process.

A circled X requires authority and specific limitations

A circled X is not a mechanic’s informal way to say “probably safe for a short trip.” It is used only through the applicable authorized process with specific limitations and required documentation. Do not create a restriction, sign for the approving person, or assume that another vehicle’s authorization applies. The interactive examples teach recognition of the concept and do not issue operational permission.

  1. Retain the original accurate deficiency description.
  2. Refer any request for restricted operation to the designated authority.
  3. Ensure required limitations and approval are recorded through the prescribed process.
  4. Operate only if authorized and within those limitations.
  5. Complete the required repair, verification, and status update afterward.

Corrective actions describe completed work

The fault describes the problem; the corrective action describes the work actually completed and the required verification. A future plan, parts request, or unperformed check must not be phrased as completed work. Required initials, signatures, dates, and approvals belong to the people who performed or authorized the corresponding action.

  1. Identify which recorded fault the action addresses.
  2. State the authorized work actually completed.
  3. Include the required verification method and result.
  4. Enter required identifying information only for your own role.
  5. Leave unresolved work open through the prescribed system.
Worked example

A training corrective-action sentence might read: “Completed the authorized repair; performed the specified lighting check and confirmed the right rear turn indicator illuminated.” Real entries must identify the actual work and reference as required.

Review legibility, accuracy, and record integrity

Use the required date format, units, identifiers, and correction method. Avoid unexplained abbreviations, ambiguous locations, copied meter readings, and blank required fields. Do not erase the history of a fault or alter someone else’s certification. Follow current instructions for correcting paper or digital records, and ask for review when a field’s meaning is unclear.

  1. Match equipment identifiers to the source record.
  2. Verify dates, meter readings, units, and technical references.
  3. Check that the fault and corrective action occupy the correct fields.
  4. Use the authorized correction method for errors.
  5. Route the record for the required review and retention.

Keep these habits

  • Write evidence, location, conditions, and the required reference.
  • Status symbols communicate decisions made under the applicable criteria and authority.
  • Never enter a future repair or unperformed check as complete.

  1. Review feedback for every missed concept.
  2. Repeat the form exercise with a different supplied scenario.
  3. Explain the difference between an observation, diagnosis, corrective action, and release.
  4. Locate the real publications and local workflow before actual work.
  5. Practice on equipment only within your training and authorization.

Keep these habits

  • Keep every record entry traceable to an observation or completed action.
  • Uncertainty and incomplete work must remain visible.
  • Course completion prepares you for supervised practice; it does not authorize maintenance or operation.

PMCS walkaround stations

A generic training sequence. Use the exact vehicle’s TM order, warnings, intervals, and limits.

Station 1 · before

Set up a safe inspection

Begin with the work area, the correct vehicle, and the applicable instructions.

  1. Match the vehicle identification and configuration to the current applicable operator TM. Open its PMCS table and read the warnings, cautions, and notes before starting.
  2. Park and secure the vehicle using its prescribed sequence. Use the parking brake, wheel chocks, and other controls where the TM or local procedure requires them.
  3. Select the PPE and tools required for the check. Identify moving traffic, hot parts, stored energy, slippery ground, and any exhaust exposure.
  4. Agree who is controlling the vehicle. Engine-off, engine-running, and functional checks are separate conditions; change conditions only when the procedure directs it.

Observe

  • Vehicle identity and manual agree.
  • The work area permits a safe inspection.
  • Required controls, PPE, and communication are in place.
Pause and escalate

Stop if the vehicle cannot be secured, the correct procedure is unavailable, or an immediate hazard makes the inspection unsafe. Notify the supervisor.

Training observation: inspection paused because the designated work area was blocked. Supervisor notified; vehicle inspection not yet completed.

Check: What is the first useful check before following a PMCS table?

  1. Verify that the manual applies to this vehicle and configuration.
  2. Use the last vehicle’s limits because it looked similar.
  3. Start the engine so every check can be performed at once.
  4. Sign the form before walking around the vehicle.

Answer: Verify that the manual applies to this vehicle and configuration. PMCS order, conditions, limits, and safety instructions come from the applicable vehicle TM. Similar-looking vehicles can have different requirements.

Station 2 · before

Walk around: body, visibility, and lights

Look for obvious changes before opening compartments or starting equipment.

  1. Follow the route in the PMCS table. Look at accessible panels, steps, handholds, mirrors, glazing, and installed safety equipment.
  2. Look for damage, loose or missing parts, obstructed visibility, and insecure doors or cargo. Do not climb on surfaces that are not intended as access points.
  3. Perform directed lamp or signal checks with a helper when needed. Keep people clear before any control is operated.
  4. Describe the exact location and observed condition. Compare it with the table’s criteria instead of deciding status from appearance alone.

Observe

  • Accessible body and access points.
  • Mirrors, glazing, and visibility.
  • Lamps and signals checked under the specified conditions.
Pause and escalate

Pause a check if safe access is unavailable or a loose component creates an immediate hazard. Report the condition and consult the TM criteria.

Training observation: right rear turn-signal lamp did not illuminate during the directed lamp check; left rear lamp illuminated.

Check: Which note best supports maintenance follow-up?

  1. Truck is bad.
  2. Lights need work.
  3. Right rear turn-signal lamp did not illuminate during the directed check.
  4. Replace the entire lighting harness immediately.

Answer: Right rear turn-signal lamp did not illuminate during the directed check. The useful note identifies a component, a location, and a directly observed symptom. It does not invent a diagnosis or repair.

Station 3 · before

Inspect tires and wheels

Observe condition and use the TM’s specified measurement method and limits.

  1. Inspect accessible tire surfaces and wheel components for the conditions named in the PMCS table, such as visible damage or missing hardware.
  2. Use the specified tool, conditions, and pressure or measurement limits when the task calls for a measurement. Do not substitute a visual estimate for a required reading.
  3. Keep clear of an obviously damaged wheel or tire assembly and notify qualified personnel. Do not attempt wheel disassembly or service as part of this general walkthrough.
  4. Record the wheel position, the observation or actual measured value, and the applicable PMCS item. An operator observation is not an instruction to tighten hardware.

Observe

  • Tire and wheel condition at each required position.
  • Required measurements with their units.
  • Missing or visibly displaced hardware, without guessing torque.
Pause and escalate

Keep people clear and report suspected unsafe tire or wheel damage. Inflation, deflation, multi-piece wheel work, and torque procedures require the applicable instructions and trained personnel.

Training observation: left front tire has visible sidewall damage. Exact location documented; supervisor notified for assessment against the TM.

Check: Where should a trainee obtain the correct tire pressure and measurement conditions?

  1. From the appearance of the tire.
  2. From a similar truck parked nearby.
  3. From a general course diagram.
  4. From the applicable vehicle instructions for the installed configuration.

Answer: From the applicable vehicle instructions for the installed configuration. Pressure and measurement conditions depend on the vehicle and tire configuration. This course intentionally supplies no universal pressure or reject limit.

Station 4 · before

Check accessible engine items and fluid levels

Identify the correct check point and check only in the conditions prescribed by the TM.

  1. Identify the correct reservoir, sight glass, dipstick, or inspection point from the manual. Read whether the check requires the engine off, a temperature condition, or a waiting period.
  2. Use approved access points and keep clear of hot surfaces and moving components. Secure an opened cover using its prescribed support.
  3. Read and record the level or condition using the specified method. Observe accessible hoses, lines, and connections without reaching into dangerous areas.
  4. Add fluid only when authorized, using the specified product and procedure. Do not mix fluids or use this course to select a lubricant.

Observe

  • Correct component and check conditions.
  • Actual level or reading where required.
  • Accessible evidence of leaks, damaged lines, or insecure covers.
Pause and escalate

Stop if a check would expose you to moving parts, unsupported covers, hot components, pressure, or an unidentified fluid. Obtain the correct procedure and assistance.

Training observation: engine-oil level read below the specified range using the TM check conditions. Supervisor notified; no fluid added in this training scenario.

Check: Why does the fluid-check condition matter?

  1. All fluids are checked in the same way.
  2. A level can be misleading or a check unsafe if temperature, engine state, or waiting time is wrong.
  3. Only the color of the fluid matters.
  4. The conditions apply only after a repair.

Answer: A level can be misleading or a check unsafe if temperature, engine state, or waiting time is wrong. A meaningful level reading depends on the method and conditions stated in the manual. Pressure and temperature can also create serious hazards.

Station 5 · before

Observe cooling components and belts

Inspect accessible condition without opening a hot pressurized system or reaching near moving parts.

  1. Confirm the engine and cooling-system conditions required by the specific check. Never remove a pressure cap from a hot pressurized system.
  2. From a safe position, inspect the accessible hoses, clamps, protective guards, and belt areas identified by the TM.
  3. Use a directed measurement or adjustment procedure only within your authorization and training. Looking at a belt does not establish its specified tension.
  4. Record the component and observable condition. Keep suspected causes separate from facts.

Observe

  • Accessible hose and connection condition.
  • Visible belt condition with moving parts stopped where required.
  • Required guards and covers in position.
Pause and escalate

Stop for hot or pressurized-system hazards, unexpected movement, missing protection, or an uncertain access method. Obtain qualified assistance.

Training observation: wetness visible at the accessible lower coolant-hose connection with the engine off. Fluid identity and applicable TM criteria require confirmation.

Check: A cooling-system check cannot be completed safely because the system is hot. What is the correct next step?

  1. Loosen the cap slightly to speed the check.
  2. Wrap the cap in any available cloth and continue.
  3. Follow the TM’s cooling and pressure precautions before continuing.
  4. Mark the check complete because the engine ran earlier.

Answer: Follow the TM’s cooling and pressure precautions before continuing. A hot cooling system can release scalding fluid and steam. The specific safe conditions must be met; an incomplete check must not be recorded as complete.

Station 6 · before

Inspect accessible electrical and battery areas

Observe accessible condition while protecting against shorts, corrosive material, and stored energy.

  1. Identify the vehicle’s electrical and battery arrangement from its instructions. Follow required shutdown, isolation, ventilation, and PPE provisions for the actual task.
  2. Inspect only the accessible items identified by the PMCS table, such as covers, restraints, cable condition, and visible corrosion.
  3. Keep conductive objects away from terminals and do not bridge connections. Disconnecting, charging, jump-starting, testing, or servicing a battery requires the separate applicable procedure.
  4. Record visible conditions without assuming that corrosion proves a failed battery or that an indicator alone proves a charging fault.

Observe

  • Accessible covers and restraints.
  • Visible cable insulation and connection condition.
  • Evidence of leakage, swelling, heat, or corrosion where safely visible.
Pause and escalate

Do not touch a damaged, leaking, unusually hot, or swollen battery. Keep clear, remove ignition sources if safe, and report it through the prescribed procedure.

Training observation: visible corrosion at the accessible battery-cable connection. No cable disconnected; supervisor notified for directed inspection.

Check: What does a general visual battery inspection authorize?

  1. Only the checks assigned by the applicable PMCS procedure and the trainee’s authorization.
  2. Any jump-start sequence found online.
  3. Shorting terminals briefly to see whether the battery has power.
  4. Replacing electrical components based on appearance alone.

Answer: Only the checks assigned by the applicable PMCS procedure and the trainee’s authorization. A visual PMCS check does not authorize battery service or troubleshooting. Those tasks have separate hazards, procedures, and qualifications.

Station 7 · before

Check the cab and occupant safety items

Prepare the operator’s position and check the assigned safety equipment.

  1. Use the vehicle’s designated steps and handholds. Maintain secure contact while entering and exiting; do not jump from the vehicle.
  2. Inspect the assigned restraints, seating, mirrors, glazing, and required onboard safety items according to the PMCS table.
  3. Confirm that loose objects will not obstruct controls or become a hazard. Make required adjustments while the vehicle is safely stationary.
  4. Learn the prescribed meaning and test behavior of warning indicators before operating the vehicle. Do not infer normal operation from a single lamp.

Observe

  • Seat and restraint condition and directed function checks.
  • Clear access to controls and required visibility.
  • Required safety equipment present and serviceable by the prescribed criteria.
Pause and escalate

Report any occupant-safety defect and use the TM’s criteria before operation. Do not bypass or improvise a repair to a safety device.

Training observation: driver restraint buckle did not latch during the prescribed check. Condition reported before any vehicle movement.

Check: When should mirrors and loose cab items be addressed?

  1. While turning out of the parking area.
  2. Before movement, with the vehicle safely stationary.
  3. Only after a warning lamp appears.
  4. At the end of the day regardless of the hazard.

Answer: Before movement, with the vehicle safely stationary. Set up a clear, safe operator position before movement. Working with loose items or adjustments while driving creates distraction and can obstruct controls.

Station 8 · before

Observe accessible chassis and driveline items

Look from safe access positions; do not turn a walkaround into unsupported under-vehicle work.

  1. Use the access method stated in the PMCS procedure. An exterior observation is different from entering beneath a vehicle or lifting it.
  2. Observe accessible frame, suspension, driveline, lines, and related components specified by the table. Look for visible damage, loose or missing parts, and new contact marks.
  3. Do not move, lift, support, or crawl under a vehicle without the applicable safe-work procedure and equipment. A hydraulic jack alone is not a safe support for under-vehicle work.
  4. Report an observable symptom and location. A mechanic’s diagnosis follows the applicable troubleshooting process.

Observe

  • Accessible structural and suspension condition.
  • Visible leaks, displaced parts, or damaged lines.
  • Evidence of contact or damage without assigning an unconfirmed cause.
Pause and escalate

Stop if inspection requires unsafe access, lifting without the correct support, or proximity to a moving driveline. Report an inaccessible check as incomplete.

Training observation: damaged protective covering visible on the accessible line near the left frame rail. Inspection restricted to safe exterior access.

Check: The PMCS item is not visible from the safe access position. What should a trainee do?

  1. Crawl under quickly while someone watches.
  2. Raise the vehicle with any available jack.
  3. Mark the item satisfactory because no problem is visible.
  4. Obtain the prescribed safe access and assistance; do not claim the check is complete.

Answer: Obtain the prescribed safe access and assistance; do not claim the check is complete. An inaccessible item is not an inspected item. The correct access and support arrangements are part of the task, not optional extras.

Station 9 · during

Observe instruments, sounds, and smells

Notice changes while keeping attention on safe operation.

  1. Carry out only the authorized operating procedure after before-operation checks and required release or dispatch steps are complete.
  2. Monitor the indicators and other conditions named by the TM. Use its specified operating range and response to warnings.
  3. Notice new vibration, unusual sounds, odors, smoke, or changes in performance. Do not keep operating merely to reproduce a potentially unsafe symptom.
  4. Keep your attention on driving. Stop at a safe location using the vehicle’s procedure before entering notes; include the conditions under which the symptom appeared.

Observe

  • Indicator or warning observed and operating condition.
  • New sound, odor, vibration, smoke, or performance change.
  • Action taken and whether operation was stopped.
Pause and escalate

Respond to warnings and unsafe behavior as the applicable operating instructions direct. If necessary, stop safely and report; do not use a phone or course screen while driving.

Training observation: warning indicator illuminated during operation. Vehicle stopped safely according to the applicable procedure; supervisor notified. Exact indicator and operating conditions recorded.

Check: When should a driver enter details in a maintenance form?

  1. While driving if the entry is short.
  2. At the next turn, before forgetting the symptom.
  3. After safely stopping; a passenger may record observations without distracting the driver.
  4. Only after attempting a repair.

Answer: After safely stopping; a passenger may record observations without distracting the driver. Documentation must not compete with vehicle control. Preserve useful observations while following the safe operating response.

Station 10 · during

Complete directed brake and steering checks

Functional checks need an approved area, the correct sequence, and an authorized operator.

  1. Read the exact functional check before performing it. Confirm the vehicle state, work area, personnel positions, and operator authorization it requires.
  2. Carry out only the TM-directed check. Do not invent a test speed, stopping distance, pressure threshold, or steering measurement from a generic example.
  3. Observe the specified response, including warnings or abnormal behavior. Stop the check if safety is uncertain.
  4. Record the specific response and conditions. A maintainer investigates the cause using approved troubleshooting; the trainee does not clear the vehicle for use.

Observe

  • Prescribed response under the specified test conditions.
  • Warning indications or abnormal behavior.
  • Whether the test was completed or stopped, and why.
Pause and escalate

If braking or steering is abnormal, follow the safe stopping procedure and report immediately. Do not continue a road test to gather more evidence.

Training observation: abnormal steering response noticed during the directed check. Check stopped safely and supervisor notified; cause not yet diagnosed.

Check: Can a general course supply one brake-test speed and stopping distance for every wheeled vehicle?

  1. No. The applicable vehicle procedure supplies the conditions and criteria.
  2. Yes, if all vehicles have the same number of wheels.
  3. Yes, provided the operator has driven before.
  4. Only the test speed needs to be universal.

Answer: No. The applicable vehicle procedure supplies the conditions and criteria. Vehicle configurations and systems differ. A generic demonstration must not replace the specific functional test and its safety controls.

Station 11 · after

Inspect after operation and describe leaks

Observe new conditions after parking without approaching hot or moving components.

  1. Park, shut down, and secure the vehicle in the prescribed sequence. Meet any cooling or waiting conditions before continuing.
  2. Complete the AFTER PMCS checks in the actual table. Observe accessible fluid evidence, loose items, damage, and changes noticed during operation.
  3. Describe leak behavior accurately: wetness without drops; drops forming but not falling; or drops falling. Identify the fluid and source only when established safely.
  4. Use the system-specific TM criteria and reporting process. Leak class alone does not determine permission to operate, and fuel or brake-fluid leaks require particular attention to their instructions.

Observe

  • Accessible new conditions after operation.
  • Location, visible behavior, and safely established fluid identity.
  • The applicable PMCS item and action taken.
Pause and escalate

Avoid contact with unknown fluids and hot components. Keep clear of suspected flammable-fluid leaks and follow the applicable hazard and reporting procedure.

Training observation: fluid drops fell from the accessible connection during observation after shutdown. Location recorded; fluid type not confirmed. Supervisor notified for assessment.

Check: Drops form at a connection but do not fall. What does that observation describe?

  1. Class I leakage, with automatic permission to operate.
  2. Class II leakage; operating status still depends on the system and applicable TM criteria.
  3. Class III leakage, regardless of the fluid involved.
  4. A confirmed failed component that the operator should replace.

Answer: Class II leakage; operating status still depends on the system and applicable TM criteria. Class II describes drops that form but do not fall. A leak description is not an operating-status decision or a diagnosis.

Station 12 · after

Document and hand over the vehicle

Make the next person’s task clear without signing for work that has not happened.

  1. Match the worksheet to the vehicle and verify required dates, meter readings, and the inspection being documented.
  2. For each new fault, use the actual PMCS item and an objective description: component, location, observed symptom, and relevant conditions. Check existing faults to avoid duplicate entries.
  3. Use the applicable status and reporting procedure. Leave corrective-action and sign-off fields to the person authorized to record the actual work and its verification.
  4. Tell the supervisor or maintenance contact what was found, what remains incomplete, and what immediate action was taken. Follow the unit’s process for the worksheet and vehicle handover.

Observe

  • Correct equipment and inspection information.
  • New faults distinguished from existing records.
  • Unresolved conditions and incomplete checks communicated.
Pause and escalate

Do not invent a correction, sign for another person, erase an unresolved fault, or assume the vehicle is released because the worksheet is complete.

Training handover: new lamp fault recorded with location and inspection item; existing tire entry reviewed; no corrective action claimed; supervisor given the worksheet.

Check: A fault has been reported, but no repair has occurred. What belongs in the corrective-action sign-off?

  1. The operator’s initials so the form looks complete.
  2. The words “fixed” because the supervisor knows about it.
  3. A guessed repair that the shop will probably perform.
  4. No invented repair or completion sign-off; follow the authorized workflow.

Answer: No invented repair or completion sign-off; follow the authorized workflow. Reporting a fault and correcting it are separate events. Completion and verification entries must reflect actual work by the authorized personnel.

Leak classification

Class I · wetness

Seepage or wetness that is not great enough to form drops.

Describe the location and fluid if safely established. This visual class alone does not establish whether operation is allowed; use the system-specific TM criteria.

Class II · drops form

Leakage great enough to form drops, but not great enough to cause the drops to fall from the item being checked.

Observe rather than guess. A formed drop is not a falling drop. Check the applicable system and TM criteria; no universal permission to operate follows from this class.

Class III · drops fall

Leakage great enough to form drops that fall from the item being checked.

Record the location and observed falling drops, then report and use the applicable TM criteria. The class does not identify the failed part or authorize a repair.

A leak class describes appearance, not permission to operate. Follow the TM’s requirements for the specific fluid, component, and condition. Do not touch unknown fluid or use a hand to find a pressurized leak.

DA Form 2404 · Equipment Inspection and Maintenance Worksheet

A manual inspection and maintenance worksheet. Match every entry to the actual equipment and current instructions. This lesson uses field names rather than claiming the layout or block numbering of the current official edition.

Organization; equipment nomenclature and model
Identify the organization and the actual equipment inspected. A familiar nickname does not replace the required equipment identification. TRAINING MOTOR POOL; instructional wheeled vehicle (fictional).
Registration / serial / national stock number identification
Use the identifier required by the form and local instructions, checked against the equipment. Do not invent an identifier or copy one from a neighboring vehicle. TRAINING-01 — synthetic classroom identifier.
Usage readings; date; type of inspection
Read the applicable meter and include its units. Enter the inspection date in the format required by the current form or system and identify the inspection being performed. Leave inapplicable meters according to the instructions. 12,340 km; 812 h; before-operation PMCS (fictional readings).
Technical manual number and date
Identify the actual applicable publication and its required edition/change information. A website or this course does not replace the equipment technical manual. TRAINING CARD A — synthetic, not an equipment TM.
Inspection / supervisor identification
Use the prescribed inspection and supervisory signature/time fields. Each person signs only for the role and work they actually performed or were authorized to verify. Training exercises do not collect real names or signatures.
Item number
Use the inspection or PMCS item reference required by the worksheet instructions. If a fault is not associated with a listed item, follow the current instructions rather than inventing a TM item number. T-A01 — item on a fictional training card only.
Status
Use the applicable maintenance symbol under the governing technical manual and maintenance instructions. Obtain help when the criteria or authority are uncertain. X is supplied by the fictional supervisor in the practice scenario.
Deficiencies and shortcomings
Describe the component, its location, and the observable condition. Add relevant measurements and units when actually obtained by an authorized procedure. Separate observations from an unconfirmed diagnosis. Right rear tire sidewall has a visible cut.
Corrective action
The responsible authorized person records the work actually performed. “Replace tire” is a proposed action, not evidence that replacement occurred. Pending handoff notes belong wherever the current worksheet and unit process require. Reporting exercises use a separate pending-action note. Correction-writing exercises supply fictional completed work and verification to document.
Initial when corrected
The authorized person initials only after the required corrective action and verification under the governing process. A trainee or operator does not initial a repair merely because a fault was reported. Reporting exercises leave initials blank; correction-writing exercises supply fictional initials for a fictional authorized corrector.

Workflow

  1. Obtain the current authorized worksheet and the technical manual for the exact equipment. Read the instructions before entering data.
  2. Match the equipment identity, meter readings, date, inspection type, and publication reference to what is actually being inspected.
  3. Perform the authorized inspection in the technical manual order. Record each new fault clearly and check for an existing record of the same condition.
  4. Enter the required item reference and status using the governing criteria. Record what you observed, where you observed it, and any reliable measurements.
  5. Report conditions requiring immediate attention. Reporting a fault, assigning a status, and authorizing operation are separate responsibilities.
  6. Route the worksheet through the prescribed maintenance process. The responsible maintainer records actual corrective action, and authorized personnel verify and close the fault as required.
  7. Review the worksheet for legibility, missing information, duplicate faults, and unsupported completion claims. Retain or file it according to the current process.

Common mistakes

  • Do not copy an example TM number, date, meter reading, fault, or signature into a real record.
  • “Bad tire” omits location and the observable condition; “needs a new tire” asserts a solution without recording the finding.
  • Do not use a form to declare that equipment is safe when the technical manual or required authority has not established that.
  • Do not erase or overwrite a status change casually. Follow the authorized correction and status-clearing instructions.

DA Form 5988-E · Equipment Maintenance and Inspection Worksheet

An equipment maintenance and inspection worksheet associated with the automated maintenance process. Its generated equipment and fault information must be checked, and new findings must reach the authorized maintenance system. Exact screen names and print layouts depend on the current implementation.

Generated equipment identification
Confirm that the worksheet belongs to the equipment in front of you. Report a mismatch; do not change an identifier just to make the sheet appear to fit. Match the equipment number and serial/registration information against the actual asset.
Usage readings and inspection information
Verify applicable miles/kilometers and hours, the inspection date, and relevant inspection information under the unit process. Keep the units attached to each reading. A distance reading of 12,340 km is not 12,340 miles.
Previously recorded faults
Review existing faults, statuses, and relevant recorded actions before writing new entries. A repeated symptom may belong to an existing open fault; a distinct new condition may require a separate record. Compare the component, location, and condition; a matching vehicle alone does not establish a duplicate.
New fault information
Record the required inspection reference and the actual condition. Where the worksheet does not provide a separate PMCS item column, include the reference as directed by the current instructions. Training item T-B02: right rear lamp lens is cracked.
Fault identifier versus PMCS item reference
A system fault number identifies a maintenance record. A PMCS item number identifies an inspection task. They serve different purposes and must not be substituted for each other. The fictional item T-B02 is not an automated fault number.
Status and maintenance action
Follow the governing status criteria and approved reporting workflow. Authorized maintenance personnel process work and update the system; writing a note on the printout does not by itself close an electronic fault. Report a new condition and route the worksheet for processing.
Correction / verification information
Record completed work and verification only through the authorized process. Keep handoff notes distinct from completion, and do not provide another person’s initials or approval. A classroom pending-action note is not a mechanic’s correction sign-off.

Workflow

  1. Obtain the worksheet produced or approved for the equipment and current inspection. Check its equipment identity before using it.
  2. Review open faults and any applicable limitations with the responsible personnel. Resolve unclear instructions before operating.
  3. Verify meter readings and perform the technical-manual inspection. Compare each finding with existing faults to avoid careless duplication.
  4. Record new findings, inspection references, and the required status according to the current instructions. Do not create a system fault number yourself.
  5. Give the worksheet and an accurate verbal handoff to the responsible maintenance personnel. Identify any condition requiring immediate action.
  6. Follow the authorized process for entering or reconciling the information in the maintenance system. Do not assume paper notes automatically updated the database.
  7. After actual maintenance and required verification, authorized personnel record completion and update status. Confirm the handoff is complete under the local process.

Common mistakes

  • A preprinted fault is not proof that the present condition has already been repaired.
  • Do not rewrite generated equipment information or delete a fault merely because it looks inconvenient.
  • Do not invent a universal GCSS-Army menu sequence: use current role-based training and unit instructions.
  • A signed dispatch, a submitted worksheet, and a closed maintenance fault are different records and decisions.

Status symbols

X — Inoperative status: A deficiency places the equipment in an inoperative status. Report the condition and follow the current technical manual and authorized maintenance process. A trainee does not clear this status by deciding that the equipment looks usable.

/ — Shortcoming: A deficiency does not place the equipment in an inoperative status under the applicable criteria. It still requires documentation and action. A slash is not a substitute for checking the technical manual or obtaining required authorization.

- — Required action not performed when due: A required inspection, component replacement, maintenance operation, or test has not been performed when due. Maintenance handoff

  1. 1. Identify the equipment and task: Match the equipment, configuration, applicable TM, PMCS interval, and worksheet. Read the task’s safety and preparation requirements.
  2. 2. Inspect under the correct conditions: Follow the table and its method. Use the required tools and measurements; distinguish a completed check from one that could not be performed safely.
  3. 3. Separate facts from diagnosis: Write what you actually saw, heard, or measured, where it occurred, and under what conditions. Do not turn a symptom into an unconfirmed cause.
  4. 4. Record and report: Use the correct inspection item and documentation workflow. Report urgent conditions promptly and avoid duplicate entries for existing faults.
  5. 5. Obtain authorized assessment: The responsible personnel apply the TM criteria, determine the required maintenance action, and follow status and dispatch procedures. A generic training result is not a release decision.
  6. 6. Perform assigned maintenance: Qualified, authorized personnel use the applicable maintenance instructions, required safety controls, tools, and parts. Record the action actually performed.
  7. 7. Verify the result: Complete the required checks and inspection after the work. Authorized personnel document verification and any permitted status changes; a repair entry alone does not prove the fault is corrected.
  8. 8. Handover and retain the record: Communicate unresolved faults and restrictions, update the authorized record system, and follow the local process for release, dispatch, and records retention.

Glossary

91B
The Army military occupational specialty for a wheeled vehicle mechanic. Actual tasks depend on training, assignment, authorization, and applicable technical instructions.
PMCS
Preventive maintenance checks and services: the inspections and services assigned by the applicable technical publication to maintain equipment and identify faults.
TM
Technical manual. The applicable current manual provides vehicle-specific procedures, warnings, inspection criteria, and technical data.
Work package
A defined portion of a technical publication addressing a task or subject. Its applicability, setup, warnings, and steps must be read together.
Maintenance allocation
The assignment of maintenance functions to authorized levels or organizations in the applicable maintenance system and technical data.
Operator check
A check assigned to the equipment operator by the applicable instructions. It does not automatically authorize maintainer-level repair.
Before-operation check
A PMCS check required before use when the applicable publication assigns that interval.
During-operation check
A required observation or check during authorized use, performed without compromising safe control.
After-operation check
A PMCS check required after use, performed under the conditions specified by the applicable publication.
Fault
A recorded abnormal equipment condition or requirement handled through the maintenance process. Its meaning and status are established using the applicable criteria and instructions.
Observation
What a person actually saw, heard, measured, or experienced, separated from an unconfirmed explanation of its cause.
Diagnosis
Identification of a fault’s cause through the authorized inspection or troubleshooting process.
Corrective action
The work actually completed to address a recorded fault, documented with required verification and identifying information.
Verification
The specified check or inspection used to establish whether the completed work achieved the required result.
Class I leak
Under a common Army convention, seepage or wetness that does not form drops. Use the applicable publication’s definitions and system criteria.
Class II leak
Under a common Army convention, leakage that forms drops but does not cause them to fall from the item being checked. Appearance alone does not authorize operation.
Class III leak
Under a common Army convention, leakage in which drops fall from the item being checked. Apply the actual system-specific criteria and required status process.
X status symbol
A common maintenance-record symbol for a deficiency that places equipment in an inoperable status. Apply current instructions and the relevant technical criteria.
Diagonal slash status symbol
A common maintenance-record symbol for a material defect other than an inoperable-status deficiency, as defined by current instructions. It is not independent permission to operate.
Horizontal dash status symbol
A common maintenance-record symbol for a required inspection or other applicable maintenance requirement that is due but not accomplished. Current instructions define its exact use.
Circled X
A maintenance-record symbol concerning operation under specifically authorized limitations. Required authority, limitations, and documentation must be established through the applicable process.
DA Form 2404
Equipment Inspection and Maintenance Worksheet. Use the current authorized form and completion instructions when the maintenance process calls for it.
DA Form 5988-E
Equipment Maintenance and Inspection Worksheet associated with automated maintenance processes. Review generated information and use the current system workflow.
DA Form 5987-E
An equipment dispatch record encountered in Army maintenance workflows. Applicability and completion follow current instructions and the unit’s authorized process.
DD Form 1970
Motor Equipment Utilization Record. Use it only where the applicable current dispatch or utilization process prescribes it.
DA Form 2408-14
Uncorrected Fault Record. Its use for unresolved or deferred faults is governed by applicable current maintenance-record instructions.
GCSS-Army
Global Combat Support System–Army. Authorized users perform maintenance and related transactions according to current training, roles, and system configuration.
Dispatch
The prescribed process for authorizing and recording equipment use. A maintenance worksheet alone does not establish dispatch authority.
Meter reading
An actual usage indication, such as an applicable mileage or operating-hours reading, recorded in the proper field and units.
Nomenclature
The standardized name or description identifying what an equipment item is; it is distinct from the individual item’s serial or registration identifier.
Energy control
The task-specific process used to control hazardous electrical, mechanical, pressure, thermal, or other energy before and during work.
Witness mark
A visual alignment mark that may show relative movement. It does not independently measure or verify torque.
Lubrication order
Technical instructions identifying required lubrication products, points, intervals, and methods for applicable equipment.
Handoff
A clear transfer of equipment information and responsibility, including findings, completed work, unresolved tasks, and required review.
