Write faults that another mechanic can act on

Turn an observation into a precise entry, apply status concepts carefully, and avoid common documentation errors.

Learning objectives

  • Write a fault using location, observation, conditions, and reference.
  • Separate fault descriptions from corrective-action entries.
  • Explain common status symbols without treating them as self-authorized operating decisions.

Use four pieces of useful information

A useful fault entry answers: where is it, what was observed, under what relevant conditions, and which required inspection reference applies? It should be concise enough to read and specific enough to find. Include measurements and units only when they were actually obtained. The exact field placement depends on the form or system; the quality of the observation matters in every format.

  1. Name the component or exact vehicle location.
  2. Describe what you saw, heard, measured, or experienced.
  3. Add relevant operating or inspection conditions.
  4. Enter the required PMCS item or technical reference in the appropriate field.
  5. Read the entry as if you had never seen the vehicle.
Worked example

Weak: “Light bad.” Better: “Right rear turn indicator did not illuminate during the prescribed lighting check.” The required item reference belongs in the form’s reference field.

Keep observation separate from diagnosis

An observation is direct evidence. A diagnosis identifies the cause after the required evaluation. An operator can accurately report that a lamp did not illuminate without asserting that the bulb failed. A mechanic can later record a confirmed cause and corrective action. Keeping those stages separate prevents guesses from becoming accepted facts in the maintenance history.

  1. Underline the facts you directly observed.
  2. Remove any unverified failed-part claim.
  3. State “source not confirmed” or similar wording when needed.
  4. Let authorized troubleshooting establish the cause.
  5. Record the confirmed cause and repair in the proper corrective-action workflow.

Understand common status symbols

Common maintenance-record symbols include X for a deficiency that places equipment in an inoperable status; a diagonal slash for a material defect other than such a deficiency; and a horizontal dash for required inspection, maintenance action, or other applicable requirement that is due but not accomplished, as defined by the current instructions. A circled X concerns operation under specifically authorized limitations. Use the exact current definitions and applicable TM criteria. These brief descriptions are not a substitute for status determination or release authority.

  1. Find the condition’s applicable technical criteria.
  2. Consult the current form or system instructions for the symbol.
  3. Have the designated personnel resolve uncertain status.
  4. Document the condition accurately without selecting a less restrictive symbol for convenience.
  5. Keep any authorized limitations explicit and within the established process.

A circled X requires authority and specific limitations

A circled X is not a mechanic’s informal way to say “probably safe for a short trip.” It is used only through the applicable authorized process with specific limitations and required documentation. Do not create a restriction, sign for the approving person, or assume that another vehicle’s authorization applies. The interactive examples teach recognition of the concept and do not issue operational permission.

  1. Retain the original accurate deficiency description.
  2. Refer any request for restricted operation to the designated authority.
  3. Ensure required limitations and approval are recorded through the prescribed process.
  4. Operate only if authorized and within those limitations.
  5. Complete the required repair, verification, and status update afterward.

Corrective actions describe completed work

The fault describes the problem; the corrective action describes the work actually completed and the required verification. A future plan, parts request, or unperformed check must not be phrased as completed work. Required initials, signatures, dates, and approvals belong to the people who performed or authorized the corresponding action.

  1. Identify which recorded fault the action addresses.
  2. State the authorized work actually completed.
  3. Include the required verification method and result.
  4. Enter required identifying information only for your own role.
  5. Leave unresolved work open through the prescribed system.
Worked example

A training corrective-action sentence might read: “Completed the authorized repair; performed the specified lighting check and confirmed the right rear turn indicator illuminated.” Real entries must identify the actual work and reference as required.

Review legibility, accuracy, and record integrity

Use the required date format, units, identifiers, and correction method. Avoid unexplained abbreviations, ambiguous locations, copied meter readings, and blank required fields. Do not erase the history of a fault or alter someone else’s certification. Follow current instructions for correcting paper or digital records, and ask for review when a field’s meaning is unclear.

  1. Match equipment identifiers to the source record.
  2. Verify dates, meter readings, units, and technical references.
  3. Check that the fault and corrective action occupy the correct fields.
  4. Use the authorized correction method for errors.
  5. Route the record for the required review and retention.

Keep these habits

  • Write evidence, location, conditions, and the required reference.
  • Status symbols communicate decisions made under the applicable criteria and authority.
  • Never enter a future repair or unperformed check as complete.
