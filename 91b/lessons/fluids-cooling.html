Fluids, leaks, and cooling systems

Check fluids under the correct conditions, describe leakage accurately, and distinguish leak appearance from operating authority.

Learning objectives

  • Use the proper conditions and method for a level check.
  • Distinguish Class I, II, and III leak appearance.
  • Explain why leak class alone cannot determine whether a vehicle may operate.

A level reading needs the right conditions

Fluid level depends on the system and inspection conditions. The TM may specify vehicle attitude, temperature, engine state, waiting time, and how to read a gauge or indicator. A reading taken under the wrong conditions can lead to overfilling or a missed problem. Identify the exact fill or check point before opening anything. Never use the course illustration to locate a real component.

  1. Identify the system and applicable level-check instruction.
  2. Establish the specified vehicle, temperature, and engine conditions.
  3. Observe warnings about heat, pressure, contamination, and moving parts.
  4. Read the correct indicator using the required method.
  5. Record or report the result and perform replenishment only if authorized.

Use only the specified product and amount

Fluids that look similar may have very different properties. Use the product specification and replenishment method required by the applicable publication. Keep containers and tools clean, protect open systems from dirt, and prevent cross-contamination. Do not mix products based on color or substitute an available lubricant without an approved instruction.

  1. Verify the required product specification and container identification.
  2. Inspect the container and dispensing equipment for contamination or damage.
  3. Use the authorized fill procedure and quantity or level criterion.
  4. Secure closures and clean incidental residue as instructed.
  5. Document consumption, service, or a recurring need for replenishment when required.

Describe leak classes carefully

A common Army leak-description convention distinguishes Class I seepage or wetness that does not form drops, Class II leakage that forms drops but does not cause them to fall, and Class III leakage in which drops fall from the item being checked. Use the definitions in the applicable publication when classifying a real finding. These descriptions concern appearance; they do not identify the fluid, locate the original source, or grant permission to operate.

  1. Observe from a safe position using the prescribed method.
  2. Determine whether there is wetness, a formed drop, or falling drops.
  3. Identify the system and source only as far as the evidence supports.
  4. Use the applicable leak definition and PMCS criteria.
  5. Record the observation and refer any uncertainty for evaluation.
Worked example

A falling drop fits the usual Class III description. Whether that condition makes this vehicle unavailable must still be evaluated using the system-specific TM criteria and required authority.

Fluid type and system matter

Do not treat every leak alike. A small amount of fluid in a safety-critical system may demand a different response from a similar-looking stain elsewhere. Fuel, brake-related fluids, coolant, lubricants, and unknown fluids have different hazards. A dark spot does not prove that the source is engine oil, and fluid can travel along a surface before it drips. Avoid touching or smelling an unknown fluid to identify it.

  1. Keep clear of hot, moving, pressurized, or otherwise hazardous areas.
  2. Record the location and appearance without guessing the source.
  3. Consult the applicable system instructions and warnings.
  4. Report an unknown or potentially hazardous leak promptly.
  5. Use the approved spill, containment, and disposal process when required.

Cooling-system checks and heat

Cooling-system inspection can include accessible hoses, connections, visible condition, and indicators when the TM directs it. A hot system may remain pressurized after shutdown. Never loosen a hot cap to speed the check, squeeze a hose to infer pressure, or continue operation based on an assumption that a warning is “just a sensor.” Fans and belts also require the specified separation and energy controls.

  1. Read the cooling-system warning and setup instructions.
  2. Wait for or establish the required safe state.
  3. Inspect the specified visible items without bypassing guards.
  4. Check the level or other condition only by the authorized method.
  5. Record a leak, damaged component, or abnormal indication and obtain the required review.

Turn a leak observation into a useful record

Separate what you directly observed from what still needs diagnosis. Include the location, fluid identity if established, leak appearance, and relevant operating conditions. If cleaning and reinspection are required to locate the source, that is a task for the authorized procedure. Do not erase the original observation merely because the surface looks dry later.

  1. Name the location as precisely as you can.
  2. State wetness, formed drops, or falling drops as observed.
  3. State the fluid identity only if established.
  4. Record the check conditions and required technical reference.
  5. Report the finding and document any authorized follow-up.
Worked example

Training wording: “Wetness visible on the lower left side of the engine compartment; no drops formed during this inspection. Fluid and source not confirmed.” This keeps observation separate from diagnosis.

Keep these habits

  • Level checks require the TM’s specified conditions.
  • Leak class describes appearance; system criteria determine the maintenance consequence.
  • Never open a hot pressurized system or identify an unknown fluid by touch.

Read alongside: The technical manual for the exact vehicle and configuration; DA Pam 750–8 — The Army Maintenance Management System (TAMMS) Users Manual.
